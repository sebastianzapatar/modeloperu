\section{Conclusions and policy implications}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We set out to establish which uncertainty governs the Peruvian power system, not to
forecast it. A system dynamics model with explicit generation and transmission
pipelines, subjected to a 129-run designed experiment over 2025--2050, answers
unambiguously.

\emph{Demand growth dominates, by an order of magnitude.} It reaches Spearman rank
correlations of $0.985$ with required transmission, $0.973$ with installed capacity
and $-0.752$ with the average price, while no other factor exceeds $0.27$ in absolute
value. A $\pm$20\,\% band on the 2050 demand level moves required transmission by
$-18.9$\,\% to $+23.8$\,\% and installed capacity by $-24.0$\,\% to $+29.3$\,\%. The
one-at-a-time and all-factors-varying evidence agree on this ranking.

\emph{Fuel-price risk is weaker than the literature on Peru implies, for a structural
reason.} Fuel prices are the weakest driver on three of four indicators, moving the
2050 average price by \SI{0.005}{\USD\per\mega\watt\hour}, because the expansion path
itself removes the exposure: thermal generation falls to
\SI{7.5}{\tera\watt\hour\per\year} of \SI{148.8}{\tera\watt\hour\per\year} by 2050.
Fuel-price risk is a near-term exposure contingent on transmission delivery rather
than an independent long-run risk.

\emph{The least-cost trajectory is the most network-intensive.} Faster demand growth
lowers the long-run average price by sustaining the price signal long enough to pull
forward low-cost wind and solar. The best case on delivered price requires
\SI{58562}{\kilo\meter} of transmission, 26.4\,\% more than the central case and
50\,\% more than the worst, with 10.3\,\% more transfer capability. For a system whose
grid is already binding, the cheap path is the one that demands most of the planner.

\emph{Lead times change when, not how much.} Transmission lead times rank last or
second-to-last on terminal indicators because the model builds much the same network
either way; the deferral surfaces as inter-zonal congestion rather than a different
system size. Terminal stocks are the wrong indicator for that effect.

\emph{The expansion is renewable without being told to be.} The central case reaches
84.9\,\% non-thermal capacity by 2050 under a profitability rule alone, with no target,
mandate or carbon price, agreeing in direction with the optimisation-based Peruvian
literature \citep{fiestas2024,delacruz2024}.

Peruvian planning attention is therefore not proportionate to the sensitivities. Effort
on fuel-price exposure buys little; effort on long-horizon demand forecasting, on the
institutional capacity to deliver transmission at the rate demand requires, and on
moving the network trigger from confirmed to anticipated projects
\citep{sauma2006,herding2024} buys a great deal.

The ranking is conditional on the sampled ranges. Storage and grid-enhancing
technologies are disabled in every run, so the flexibility--network substitution is
absent and the transmission requirement of the high-demand case is, if anything,
overstated; retirements are exogenous, the dispatch uses one representative day per
month, and the distributions are ranges of admissibility rather than estimated
densities. Each is documented with its consequence in the supplementary material.
Three extensions follow: enabling storage and grid-enhancing technologies under the
same design; replacing terminal stocks with congestion-hours, curtailment and unserved
energy to capture the timing effect of lead times; and applying the design to Brazil
and Chile with the same model structure, to separate what is Peruvian from what is a
property of longitudinal systems generally.
