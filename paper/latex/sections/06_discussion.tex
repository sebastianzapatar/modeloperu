\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Demand growth as the governing uncertainty}

That demand dominates is structurally explicable. It enters at the root of every chain:
it sets the adequacy trigger, the residual demand the dispatch clears and hence the
price signal driving the profitability trigger, and the zonal peaks that drive
demand-connection network. Fuel prices enter only at the dispatch, and in a system that
ends 85\,\% non-thermal a cost shift on the shrinking thermal margin has little
leverage --- the rank correlation of $-0.014$ is what one should expect once the
marginal technology is no longer the one burning the fuel.

The reading is uncomfortable: demand growth is the one driver planning does not
control and forecasting handles poorly, and our $\pm$20\,\% range is arguably
conservative for an emerging economy with incomplete electrification and potential
industrial loads. Long-horizon demand uncertainty \citep{alvarez2026} is therefore the
main lever on the quality of the answer, not a peripheral refinement.

\subsection{The price result and what it does not say}

The finding needs careful statement. It concerns a long-run \emph{average} under an
investment rule responding to profitability: faster demand sustains the price signal
long enough for low-cost capacity to clear the threshold earlier and in greater volume,
and that capacity then depresses the marginal price. It does not say demand growth is
costless --- the same runs require 26\,\% more transmission and 35\,\% more generation
capacity, and the model prices neither the capital at risk nor the land and social cost
of that build-out --- nor anything about short-run prices: Figure~\ref{fig:price} shows
the high-demand case \emph{above} the others through the late 2020s.

Three structural caveats bound it. The model has no endogenous retirement, so the mix
shifts by addition rather than replacement. Storage and grid-enhancing technologies
are disabled in every run, so the substitution between flexibility and network
documented by \citet{sousa2024} and \citet{sadeghian2026} is absent; enabling them
would plausibly reduce the transmission requirement of the high-demand case and weaken
exactly the corollary we emphasise. And the dispatch uses one representative day per
month, which under-represents sustained scarcity.

\subsection{Delays, and what the indicators miss}

Lead times rank second and third, well behind demand. We do not read this as evidence
that delays are unimportant for Peru.

What the result says is that, over a $\pm$20\,\% band around current practice, lead
times do not change the \emph{size} of the system built by 2050 --- not that they leave
its condition unchanged along the way. Section~\ref{sec:results:where} shows the
mechanism: longer pipelines defer reinforcements, and in a series topology the deferral
surfaces as inter-zonal congestion rather than a different terminal capacity. An
indicator measured at January 2050 is poorly suited to capture that, which is a
limitation of our reporting as much as a property of the system; a study aimed at
reliability would need congestion-hours, curtailment and unserved energy.

There is also a range effect. A $\pm$20\,\% band is plausible for incremental
administrative improvement but not for institutional failure: the literature on delay
causes \citep{sun2026,boateng2026} describes multi-year slippage on individual
projects, well outside $\pm$20\,\% of a planned stage. Our ranking is conditional on
the ranges in Table~\ref{tab:factors}, as any sensitivity ranking must be.

\subsection{Fuel-price exposure reconsidered}

Peru's gas dependence is the feature most often identified as its principal
vulnerability \citep{rudnick2019,enerdata2024,colinacalvo2024}, and our results qualify
that view temporally rather than substantively. Fuel prices are the weakest driver on
every indicator because the model's own trajectory removes the exposure: by 2050
thermal plant supplies \SI{7.5}{\tera\watt\hour\per\year} of
\SI{148.8}{\tera\watt\hour\per\year}. Fuel-price risk is therefore a near-term exposure
that the expansion path itself retires --- provided the path is delivered. If the
network cannot be built at the rate the investment rule assumes, the system stays
thermal for longer and the exposure persists. Read that way, fuel-price risk in Peru is
a \emph{consequence} of transmission delivery rather than an independent risk to hedge
separately, which is a different prescription from the usual one.

\subsection{Renewable integration and coordination}

The central case reaches 84.9\,\% non-thermal capacity by 2050 with no renewable
target, driven by profitability alone --- a stronger statement about the
competitiveness of Peruvian wind and solar than a target-constrained model could make,
and one that aligns with the optimisation-based findings of \citet{fiestas2024} and
\citet{delacruz2024} from a different methodological direction. Where the two diverge
is on what getting there costs: an optimisation model coordinates generation siting and
network expansion by construction, while ours does not, because the Peruvian
institutional arrangement does not. \citet{herding2024} quantify the prize for closing
that gap --- around 10\,\% lower investment and up to 30\,\% less network expansion ---
and \citet{sauma2006} formalise the proactive-versus-reactive distinction underlying
it. Peru is firmly on the reactive side, and the cost of remaining there rises with
demand growth. Complementary options attack the constraint from the demand side:
distributed generation reduces the transfers a longitudinal grid must support
\citep{antoniolli2022,wu2025,sheng2026}, and grid-enhancing technologies raise corridor
capability without new right-of-way \citep{sadeghian2026}. Both are represented but
disabled here; quantifying them is the obvious next study, and our results identify
where the gain would be largest --- the Z2--Z3 corridor under high demand.

\subsection{Policy implications and comparison}

Four implications follow. \emph{Transmission delivery capacity is the planning variable
to protect}: demand governs the outcome and is outside the planner's control, while the
least-cost outcome is the most network-intensive, so under-building the grid forecloses
the cheap trajectory. \emph{Anticipatory network planning has measurable value},
since the reactive trigger plus the lead-time asymmetry is a structural bottleneck
rather than a parameter choice \citep{sauma2006,herding2024}. \emph{Demand forecasting
deserves the effort currently given to fuel-price hedging}, given a ratio of roughly
1:900 in rank correlation between the two. And \emph{restoring a predictable
procurement signal matters more than the price it clears}, since the profitability
trigger responds to a sustained signal that the suspended auction mechanism removes
\citep{campodonico2022,torres2026,delrio2026}.

Peru's series topology drives the result and is unusual regionally: Brazil's meshed
system offers alternatives when a corridor binds, so the constraint appears as regional
heterogeneity \citep{dasilva2025} or curtailment \citep{herrera2019} rather than a
single critical path, while Chile --- also longitudinal --- is the more instructive
comparison and has moved further from a similar base \citep{reyes2025}. The common
regional thread is institutional \citep{guliev2022,sempertegui2017,boateng2026}, and
our finding should not be generalised to systems with weaker institutions or wider
delay distributions than we sampled.

\subsection{Methodological implications}

Pairing a system dynamics model with a designed experiment changes what the model can
claim: named scenarios show what a model does but cannot separate a structural result
from an artefact of one input assumption. Here it turned a plausible narrative --- that
a gas-dependent system faces fuel-price risk --- into a measured ranking that
contradicts it, for 129 mechanisable runs with the design fixed before execution.
Reporting both one-at-a-time and all-factors-varying evidence is worth the redundancy,
since their agreement is what makes the ranking defensible. The execution
infrastructure --- resumable runs, an archived design, explicit checks that each run
produced data --- was likewise not incidental: those checks caught silent failures that
would otherwise have entered the results as missing data rather than as errors.
