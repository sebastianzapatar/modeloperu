\section{The Peruvian power system}
\label{sec:peru}

\subsection{Overview and institutional structure}
\label{sec:peru:overview}

In the late 1980s Peru's power sector was in crisis, driven by a broader
macroeconomic collapse and poorly performing utilities. The response was a
structural reform: the country moved from an integrated state system to a
competitive wholesale market with marginal nodal prices, capacity payments and
bilateral contracts, which delivered efficiency gains, new installed capacity and a
more secure supply \citep{rudnick2019}. The Electric Concessions Law of 1992
unbundled what had been a single chain into generation, transmission and
distribution, opening the sector to private investment. A subsequent wave of
reforms, including the supply auctions introduced from 2006, consolidated
productivity gains, with clear efficiency improvements in distribution after
restructuring \citep{perezreyes2009}. Challenges persist in state-owned companies
and in rural coverage.

\subsection{Generation mix and dependence on thermal generation}
\label{sec:peru:mix}

The Peruvian generation matrix is dominated by hydropower and natural-gas-based
thermal generation. By 2015 the major technologies were roughly 50\,\% hydro and
45\,\% natural gas, with thermal sources overall --- gas, coal and oil ---
accounting for approximately 41\,\% of generation by 2017 \citep{rudnick2019}. This
gas dependence is structural rather than incidental: over half of all natural gas
consumed in the country goes to power plants \citep{enerdata2024}, which makes the
electricity sector the primary driver of gas demand and leaves it exposed to price
shocks and supply disruptions.

Despite substantial potential in solar, wind, geothermal, biomass and hydroelectric
resources, non-hydro renewables have remained marginal. Wind, solar and biomass
together contributed only 2.8\,\% of net generation in 2017 \citep{rudnick2019}, and
by 2022 only around 5\,\% of electricity generation came from non-conventional
renewable sources \citep{campodonico2022}. There has been diversification into solar
photovoltaics, wind, biomass and small hydro, but thermal dependence on natural gas
remains the defining structural feature of the matrix.

\subsection{Renewable expansion potential}
\label{sec:peru:potential}

Peru's resource base is substantial --- solar radiation, coastal and highland wind,
geothermal activity, biomass and hydroelectric potential are all present in
significant measure --- yet little of it has been converted into generation
capacity. \citet{fiestas2024} find that raising non-conventional renewables to
28.26\,\% of the matrix would cut gas consumption by \SI{2.04}{\kilo\meter\cubed}
and avoid \SI{4.99}{\mega\tonne} CO$_2$-eq annually while reducing overall system
costs. Longer-range scenarios to 2050 point the same way: prioritising renewable
integration alongside transmission and distribution efficiency improvements
produces significantly lower emissions than the baseline trajectory
\citep{delacruz2024}. The economic and environmental case is, in other words,
reasonably well established.

What is less clear is how to get there. The auction mechanism that for over a
decade provided a structured channel for renewable investment has been suspended,
leaving developers without a reliable off-take signal \citep{campodonico2022}. The
surrounding conditions make this harder: excess installed capacity depresses
wholesale prices, economic growth has slowed, and the regulatory framework is
fragmented across institutions with overlapping mandates. On top of that, the grid
was not built with variable renewables in mind, and the mismatch between where the
best resources are and where demand is concentrated remains an unresolved physical
constraint \citep{torres2026}. The international evidence suggests this combination
is not unusual: \citet{boateng2026} show that institutional quality conditions how
strongly renewable deployment responds to otherwise identical incentives, and
\citet{delrio2026} find that in Latin America the cost of capital, rather than
resource quality or levelised cost, determines which projects are financed.

\subsection{Transmission corridors and the zonal structure}
\label{sec:peru:zones}

Transmission infrastructure is both a current bottleneck and a strategic lever for
Peru's energy transition. Within the national system, congestion management has
become more sophisticated, with $n-1$ contingency screening alongside demand,
generation, fuel-price and weather projections, enabling early detection of
long-term congestion points and supporting a network that can better accommodate
renewable sources while reducing operational costs \citep{valdivia2024}.

The model represents the system as three zones connected in series, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:map}. This is not a simplification imposed for tractability but a
representation of the physical topology: Peru's main grid runs longitudinally, and
the consequence is that the southern zone reaches the rest of the system only
through the centre. Table~\ref{tab:zones} reports the January 2025 starting
condition. The centre zone (Z2) holds 60\,\% of installed capacity, the two
corridors Z1--Z2 and Z2--Z3 provide \SI{900}{\mega\watt} and
\SI{700}{\mega\watt} of transfer capability respectively, and there is no direct
Z1--Z3 path. A series topology of this kind has no redundancy: a constraint on the
central corridor isolates a third of the system, and every inter-zonal transfer
between the extremes consumes capacity on both corridors.

At the regional level, analyses of Andean interconnection indicate that technically
and economically feasible links among Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru
would generate long-run economic and environmental benefits. These opportunities
remain largely unexecuted. Unlike Central America, which has advanced toward a
regional electricity market through supranational institutions, the Andean region
has stalled, primarily because member states are reluctant to cede regulatory
sovereignty, despite transmission infrastructure that makes integration technically
viable \citep{sempertegui2017,guliev2022}. The gap between technical feasibility and
political will, rather than any engineering constraint, is the principal barrier.

\subsection{Planning challenges and infrastructure delays}
\label{sec:peru:delays}

The gains from Peru's 1990s reform were real but uneven, and over time the limits
of the institutional design became apparent. Once the initial wave of privatisation
ran its course, new investment in generation and transmission slowed considerably,
a sign that the regulatory framework was not generating the long-term certainty
that infrastructure capital requires \citep{rudnick2019}. State-owned distributors
have continued to underperform, rural electrification remains incomplete, and
government has periodically intervened outside market mechanisms in ways that
distort price signals.

These shortcomings matter now, as the country attempts to expand its renewable
base. The suspension of the auction mechanism has hit onshore wind hardest:
projects conceived for a structured procurement environment are instead being asked
to compete in a spot market under considerable price uncertainty
\citep{torres2026}. Beyond procurement, wind development faces a geographic
difficulty --- the best resources are far from consumption, and the grid lacks the
flexibility to bridge that gap. Taken together, fossil-fuel dependence and a
renewable share of roughly 5.5\,\% of total energy leave Peru exposed to external
price swings while limiting access to cleaner supply, a situation that will not
improve without more deliberate institutional backing \citep{colinacalvo2024}.

This is the feature of the Peruvian case that motivates the modelling choice in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. Lead times here are not an engineering parameter but
an institutional outcome, and they differ systematically between generation, which
responds to a market signal, and transmission, which is planned centrally and
delivered on a longer clock. Representing that asymmetry explicitly is what allows
the model to be asked which of the two actually binds.

\subsection{Relevance of the Peruvian case}
\label{sec:peru:relevance}

Peru occupies a distinctive position in comparative analyses of Latin American
energy transitions, functioning simultaneously as a reference case for successful
market reform and as a cautionary example of its limits. The post-1992
restructuring achieved genuine competition, lower wholesale prices and improved
security of supply, but at the cost of increasing fossil-fuel dependence and a high
reserve margin that raises questions about long-term allocative efficiency
\citep{rudnick2019}. Peru was also among the regional pioneers of
technology-specific renewable auctions, which produced price reductions of 78\,\%
for solar and 53\,\% for wind and demonstrated that competitive procurement can
scale non-conventional renewables in emerging markets.

Early policy innovation has not, however, translated into a consolidated transition
strategy. Legal reforms have accumulated incrementally rather than forming a unified
framework, and no comprehensive national decarbonisation plan exists --- a gap
visible when Peru is set against Chile, which started from a comparable liberalised
base and has moved considerably further \citep{reyes2025}. Peru's situation reflects
a tension that runs through the region: the same country holds major hydrocarbon
reserves, Camisea being the most significant, alongside world-class solar, wind,
biomass and green hydrogen potential. How governments navigate that duality, and
the related choices about gas as a transitional fuel, hydropower expansion and
regional grid integration, will do much to determine what Latin America's energy
system looks like in the long run \citep{guliev2022}.
