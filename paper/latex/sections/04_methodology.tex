\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Modelling approach}
\label{sec:method:approach}

The question posed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} --- which uncertainty governs
the trajectory of the Peruvian system --- requires two things that are usually
provided separately. It requires a model whose behaviour arises from the structure
of the decision process rather than from an optimality assumption, because the
phenomenon of interest is the mismatch between a decentralised generation market
and a centrally planned network. And it requires a designed experiment over the
inputs, because a ranking cannot be read off a handful of named scenarios.

We therefore combine a system dynamics simulation model with a Latin-hypercube
designed experiment. The simulation runs monthly from January 2025 to January 2050
(300 months, integration step \SI{0.25}{\month}), with an hourly dispatch
represented inside each month by one representative day of 24 hourly levels. The
experiment comprises 129 runs: one central case, two one-at-a-time runs per factor
for the tornado diagram (eight), and 120 Monte Carlo samples in which all four
factors vary simultaneously.

The division of labour between the two is deliberate. The one-at-a-time runs
isolate each factor against a fixed reference, which is what makes a tornado bar
attributable to that factor and nothing else; their limitation is that they say
nothing about interaction. The Monte Carlo ensemble moves everything at once, which
is what the percentile envelopes and the rank correlations require; its limitation
is that an individual factor's effect is no longer separable by construction and
must be inferred statistically. Reporting both, and checking that they agree on the
ranking, is stronger than either alone.

\subsubsection{Generation: investment, pipeline and commissioning}
\label{sec:method:gen}

Generation capacity is a stock fed by a pipeline of projects that must pass through
licensing and construction before they can be commissioned. Writing
$N_{z,g}(t)$ for the capacity committed in month $t$:

\begin{align}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{\text{lic}}_{z,g}}{\mathrm{d}t}
    &= N_{z,g}(t) - \mathcal{D}\!\left[N_{z,g}, L^{\text{lic}}_{z,g}\right](t),
    \label{eq:lic}\\[2pt]
  \frac{\mathrm{d}Q^{\text{con}}_{z,g}}{\mathrm{d}t}
    &= \mathcal{D}\!\left[N_{z,g}, L^{\text{lic}}_{z,g}\right](t)
     - \mathcal{D}\!\left[N_{z,g}, L^{\text{lic}}_{z,g} + L^{\text{con}}_{z,g}\right](t),
    \label{eq:con}\\[2pt]
  \frac{\mathrm{d}K_{z,g}}{\mathrm{d}t}
    &= \min\!\Big\{
         \mathcal{D}\!\left[N_{z,g}, L^{\text{tot}}_{z,g}\right](t),\;
         \Phi_{z}(t)
       \Big\},
    \label{eq:cap}
\end{align}

where $\mathcal{D}[\cdot,L]$ is a material delay of duration $L$ and
$L^{\text{tot}}_{z,g} = \big(L^{\text{lic}}_{z,g} + L^{\text{con}}_{z,g}\big)\,
(1+\Delta_g)$ is the total lead time including the lead-time factor. The term
$\Phi_{z}(t)$ in \eqref{eq:cap} is the headroom the delivered network allows in
zone $z$: capacity that has finished construction cannot be commissioned until
there is transmission to evacuate it. Equation~\eqref{eq:cap} is the first of the
two feedbacks drawn dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:model}, and it is what couples the
generation market to the network plan.

Investment $N_{z,g}(t)$ is triggered by two distinct mechanisms. The first is
adequacy: when the expected reserve margin at horizon $f$ falls below the desired
margin $\bar m$, the shortfall is converted into a capacity requirement. The second
is profitability: for each zone--technology pair the model computes a net present
value over a 25-year economic life, assuming the plant is the only entrant and that
current plus already-committed capacity persists, using CAPEX, fixed O\&M, fuel and
firm-capacity (ENFICC) parameters from \citet{nrel2024}. Options whose profitability
index exceeds $\Pi^{*}$ are admitted. Admitted options are then allocated across
technologies by a logit share with scale $\theta$, which represents the dispersion
of investor beliefs rather than a single optimising agent --- the point at which the
model departs most clearly from a GEP formulation.

Decisions are taken on \emph{expected} rather than current state. Expected capacity
at horizons of 3, 5, 7 and 9 years includes capacity already in the pipeline, so
investors and planners are assumed to know the construction times they face. This
is the second dashed feedback in Figure~\ref{fig:model} and it is what prevents the
model from over-investing in response to a deficit that is already being remedied.

\subsubsection{Transmission: three categories, three triggers}
\label{sec:method:trans}

Transmission expansion is disaggregated into three categories because in this
system they respond to different drivers and carry different lead times.

\emph{Transmission for demand.} Peak demand growth in a zone requires network
inside that zone, at an empirical intensity $\kappa_d$ [\si{\kilo\meter\per\mega\watt}]:
$T^{d}_{z}(t) = \kappa_d\,\hat D_z(t)$, with $\hat D_z$ the zonal peak. This
category carries no construction delay, because the model does not represent
intra-zonal networks and a delay there would not affect inter-zonal operation.

\emph{Transmission for generation.} Each new plant requires the network without
which it could not reach commercial operation, at intensity $\kappa_g$. The trigger
is the formal identification of a project at a planning horizon, which makes this
category structurally \emph{reactive}: it responds to confirmed projects rather
than anticipating them, and because transmission lead times exceed generation lead
times, this is a principal source of the bottleneck the model is built to study.

\emph{Transmission for interconnection.} Corridor reinforcement is driven by the
dispatch. At each step the dispatch identifies zonal surpluses and deficits and the
implied inter-zonal transfers; where the required transfer exceeds the delivered
corridor capability $P_i(t)$, a reinforcement is triggered. If the shortfall is at
most a share $\alpha$ of current capability it may be met by a grid-enhancing
technology, which has a shorter pipeline; otherwise a new line is required.
Increments are bounded below and above by $F_{\min}$ and $F_{\max}$, which
represents the lumpiness of real reinforcements.

Transmission projects pass through a four-stage pipeline --- public tender,
investor selection, licensing, construction --- each stage scaled by $(1+\Delta_t)$.
The 12-month planning cycle is deliberately \emph{not} scaled: the model uses it as
the period of a modulo operation that sets when plans are revised, so scaling it
would shift the planning calendar rather than delay projects.

\subsubsection{Dispatch and prices}
\label{sec:method:dispatch}

Each month the model dispatches one representative day of 24 hourly levels by merit
order. Each zone--technology pair is represented by one plant with an offer price
and an hourly availability profile; reservoir hydro offers between a minimum and a
scarcity price as a function of its storage level, which is what gives hydrology a
price signal rather than merely an energy constraint. This is a reduced-form
representation of reservoir operation: a full treatment would optimise releases
against inflow forecasts and environmental constraints \citep{schaffer2026}, which
matters more for operational studies than for the expansion question asked here.
Two dispatches are computed:
an unconstrained one, used to size the transfers the network would ideally carry
and hence to detect reinforcement needs, and a constrained one that respects
delivered corridor capability and yields congestion, zonal prices and the realised
generation mix.

The delivered price reported in this paper is the country average electricity
tariff, obtained from the zonal tariffs over the active zones. Because the price
oscillates month to month with hydrology, the reported indicator is the mean over
the final twelve months of the horizon rather than a single month; a single month
is not representative of the state the system has reached.

The model also supports an external dispatch path in which the constrained
dispatch is solved as a linear program with PyPSA \citep{brown2018} through the
Vensim DLL. All results reported here use the internal rule-based dispatch, for
consistency with the central case against which the ensemble is compared.

\subsubsection{Distributed resources and storage}
\label{sec:method:der}

Distributed PV adoption is represented as a diffusion process driven by the gap
between the levelised cost of distributed generation and the retail tariff,
moderated by a potential market and a social-influence term --- the structure used
in the system dynamics adoption literature \citep{oyarzun2026} and consistent with
the empirical role documented for prosumers in neighbouring systems
\citep{antoniolli2022,wu2025}. Battery storage, where enabled, arbitrages between
the cheapest and the most expensive hour of the representative day. Both reduce the
residual demand seen by the dispatch and hence, indirectly, the inter-zonal
transfers the network must support --- the substitution between storage and network
quantified by \citet{sousa2024}.

In the configuration used for every run reported here, system-level storage and
grid-enhancing technologies are \emph{disabled} at the switch level, as in the
shipped baseline, while distributed resources remain endogenous. PV-plus-storage
\emph{technologies} can still be selected by the investment rule and do appear in
the results. This is stated explicitly because it bounds the interpretation: the
study quantifies uncertainty around the current policy configuration, not the
effect of deploying storage or GETs.

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{sec:method:design}

\subsubsection{Factors and ranges}

Four drivers are varied. They were selected because each is uncertain, each is
outside the planner's direct control on the relevant horizon, and each has been
argued in the literature to be a principal risk for a system with Peru's
characteristics.

\begin{table}[tb]
  \centering
  \caption{Uncertain factors, ranges, and the model quantity each one scales.
  Ranges are ranges of admissibility, not estimated distributions.}
  \label{tab:factors}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{52mm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Factor & Range & Model quantity \\
    \midrule
    Demand growth $g_D$ & $-20$ / $+20$\,\% &
      multiplier on national demand, equal to 1 in January 2025 and
      $1+g_D$ in January 2050, so only the growth path changes \\
    Generation lead time $\Delta_g$ & $-20$ / $+20$\,\% &
      licensing plus construction time of every generation technology \\
    Transmission lead time $\Delta_t$ & $-20$ / $+20$\,\% &
      tender, investor selection, licensing and construction of lines and GETs \\
    Fuel price $c_f$ & $-15$ / $+15$\,\% &
      fuel cost of every technology that burns fuel \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two conventions require comment. First, the demand factor is a \emph{growth-path}
factor, not a level shift: the multiplier is unity at the start of the horizon and
reaches $1+g_D$ at the end, so January 2025 demand is untouched and only the rate of
growth varies. This is the correct construction for an expansion study, where the
quantity in question is how fast load grows, not how large it is today. Second,
generation lead times are expressed relative to the \emph{workbook base case}
rather than to the planned time. The Peruvian data set carries an ``other delays''
fraction of $-0.2$, meaning realised delay is 80\,\% of planned; the factor is
therefore applied as $(1+\text{base})(1+\text{change})-1$, giving $-0.36$ at the low
end and $-0.04$ at the high end. This is the same convention used for transmission,
which is what makes the two delay bars of the tornado comparable with each other.

Distributions are uniform and independent. There is no information that would
justify another shape, and we are explicit that these are ranges of admissibility
rather than estimated distributions: the ranking is robust to this choice, the
absolute width of the percentile band is not.

\subsubsection{Sampling}

The Monte Carlo sample is a Latin hypercube \citep{mckay1992}: each factor's range
is divided into as many equal intervals as there are samples, one value is drawn at
random inside each interval, and the intervals of the factors are paired at random.
This gives even marginal coverage with far fewer runs than simple random sampling,
which matters when a single run costs several minutes of simulation. The design is
generated from a fixed seed and written to disk before any simulation starts, so
the experiment is fully specified independently of its execution.

The realised design has a maximum absolute correlation between factors of $0.181$
and a mean of $0.076$. Near-orthogonality matters because the rank correlations
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results:ranking} are only attributable to a single
factor when the factors are close to independent.

\subsubsection{Central, best and worst case}
\label{sec:method:scenarios}

We report three trajectories in detail, and the basis for choosing them is stated
rather than left implicit.

The \textbf{central case} is the system run with every factor at its reference
value --- the configuration of the input data set as compiled from Peruvian sources.
It is not a forecast and it is not the ensemble median; it is the reference against
which every other run is measured, and it is reported because the tornado
diagram's bars are defined as departures from it.

The \textbf{best} and \textbf{worst} cases are selected from the 120 Monte Carlo
runs on a single declared metric: the lowest and the highest country average
electricity price, averaged over the final twelve months. A single declared
criterion keeps the selection auditable, which is the point of reporting two runs
in full; a weighted composite over several indicators would make the choice depend
on weights that have no defensible basis. The consequence --- that ``best'' on
price need not be best on every other indicator --- is not a defect of the
criterion but a result, and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:results:extremes}.

\subsection{Implementation and validation}
\label{sec:method:validation}

The model is implemented in Vensim DSS \citep{ventana2024} and executed through its
DLL from a Python driver that applies each sample, runs the simulation, and
extracts the reported variables. Each uncertain factor enters the model as a named
constant multiplying the corresponding structural equation, with a default value
that reproduces the unmodified model exactly. The complete driver, the design and
the analysis code are available with the paper.

Model confidence is established by the standard system dynamics battery
\citep{barlas1996,oliva2004,richardson2024} rather than by a single fit statistic,
which is the appropriate standard for a model used to explore structure rather than
to forecast. The tests are reported in full in Supplementary
Material~S3; in summary:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Structure verification.} Every feedback loop is matched to a
        documented decision process and a source.
  \item \emph{Parameter verification.} Every parameter is reported with its value,
        unit, source and admissible range (Supplementary Material~S2).
  \item \emph{Dimensional consistency.} The model passes Vensim's units check.
  \item \emph{Extreme-condition tests.} Six tests, including zero demand growth,
        zero construction time and unbounded lead times, each with the behaviour
        that structural soundness requires.
  \item \emph{Behaviour reproduction.} The central case reproduces the documented
        reference trajectory of the Peruvian system, and the published four-factor
        model reproduces the central case to \SI{63.85}{\giga\watt} and
        \SI{46341.23}{\kilo\meter} at January 2050, matching the reference exactly.
  \item \emph{Behaviour sensitivity.} The designed experiment of
        Section~\ref{sec:method:design} is itself the sensitivity test, and the
        convergence of the reported percentiles is checked in
        Section~\ref{sec:results:ranking}.
\end{itemize}
