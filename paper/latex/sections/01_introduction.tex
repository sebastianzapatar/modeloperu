\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Expansion planning rests on numbers nobody knows. What fuel will cost a decade out,
how fast demand will grow, and how late projects will actually come online are all
uncertain, and each is routinely handled by picking a central value and running a
scenario around it. That practice is defensible only if the uncertainties are of
comparable weight. When one dominates, effort spent hedging the others is
misallocated, and the planning question changes from \emph{what might happen} to
\emph{which unknown is worth managing}.

Peru is a useful place to ask that question. Its generation mix is still built on
hydropower and natural gas, with non-conventional renewables at roughly 5\,\% of
generation despite a resource base that modelling work repeatedly finds economically
attractive \citep{fiestas2024,delacruz2024}. Its transmission network is
longitudinal: three load zones strung in series, with the best wind and solar
resources systematically distant from the demand they would serve
\citep{valdivia2024}. And its institutional record is mixed in an instructive way ---
the 1992 restructuring produced genuine competition and improved security of supply
\citep{rudnick2019,perezreyes2009}, and Peru pioneered technology-specific renewable
auctions, but that mechanism has since been suspended and investment in both
generation and transmission slowed once privatisation ran its course. The country
holds, simultaneously, a strong resource endowment, a grid not built for it, and a
planning process whose delivery times are themselves uncertain.

Most of the modelling literature addresses this with optimisation. Generation and
transmission expansion planning, jointly or separately, are mature fields
\citep{gacitua2018,babatunde2020,herding2024,tao2025,valipour2026}. These models are
indispensable for identifying what an efficient expansion path would look like, but
they answer a normative question under an assumption of perfect rationality and a
single optimising agent. The system they describe is not the system that exists: in a
liberalised market, generation investment is decentralised and driven by expected
profitability, transmission is planned centrally on a longer clock, and the gap
between the two is bridged by neither \citep{dyner2001,herrera2019}. It is in that
gap that delays, congestion and stranded capacity appear.

System dynamics was developed for exactly this situation --- feedback, accumulation
and delay \citep{forrester1997,sterman2000} --- and has a long record of reproducing
the investment cycles and capacity gaps that optimisation abstracts away
\citep{ford2001,arango2007,assili2008,zapata2018}. What it is less often asked to do
is to \emph{quantify}, rather than illustrate, the relative weight of the
uncertainties driving it. A single simulation, or a handful of named scenarios, shows
what the model does; it does not show which input the answer depends on.

This paper closes that gap for Peru. We build a system dynamics model of the Peruvian
system, coupled to a congestion-aware dispatch layer, with an explicit regulatory and
construction pipeline for both generation and transmission. We subject it to a
designed experiment of 129 simulations over 2025--2050: a central case, eight
one-at-a-time runs isolating each driver, and 120 Latin-hypercube Monte Carlo samples
in which four drivers move at once --- fuel prices, demand growth, and project lead
times on the generation and the transmission side.

The contribution is threefold: a \textbf{quantified ranking} of the uncertainties
rather than an illustration of them, reported as one-at-a-time sensitivities and rank
correlations with a convergence check; a \textbf{transmission-explicit representation}
of a longitudinal system, in which grid built for demand, for generation connection and
for interconnection each carry their own trigger and lead time, only the third being
constrained by the series topology; and a \textbf{reproducible artefact}, with model,
data, design, code and a notebook regenerating every figure openly available and the
validation battery and full parameter set as supplementary material.

The central result bears directly on policy and is counter-intuitive. Higher demand
growth does not raise the long-run average price in this system; it lowers it, by
pulling forward low-cost renewable capacity that the central case defers. The
least-cost trajectory is consequently the most transmission-intensive, requiring about
a quarter more network. For a system whose grid is already the binding constraint, that
reframes the planning problem: the risk to manage is not fuel-price exposure but
whether transmission can be delivered fast enough to serve demand growth that is itself
the dominant unknown.
