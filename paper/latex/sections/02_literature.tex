\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Transmission planning under the energy transition}
\label{sec:lit:planning}

Transmission is the part of the power system where the energy transition is most
visibly constrained. The resources that decarbonise a system are located where the
wind blows and the sun shines, not where load is concentrated, so every increment
of renewable capacity implies an increment of network; and the network is planned
centrally, built slowly, and remunerated under rules written for a different
generation fleet.

The modelling response has largely been optimisation. Transmission expansion
planning (TEP) treats a central planner maximising social welfare subject to
network constraints, and the literature has extended it steadily to accommodate
the transition: stochastic formulations that make renewable hosting capacity an
explicit objective \citep{valipour2026}, multi-stage investment games for offshore
grids \citep{tao2025}, and allocation models that coordinate renewable siting with
inter-regional transfer capability \citep{zhao2026}. Joint generation and
transmission formulations (GTEP) go further, and the coordination gain they
identify is substantial: \citet{herding2024} find that coordinating the two
reduces total investment by around 10\,\% and network expansion by up to 30\,\%
when distributed generation is allowed to displace transfers. Reviews of the field
\citep{gacitua2018,babatunde2020} document both its maturity and its central
assumption --- a single rational agent optimising a well-posed objective.

That assumption is where the difficulty lies for the question we ask. In a
liberalised market the generation investor and the network planner are different
agents, acting on different information, over different horizons, and the
literature itself notes that optimisation struggles to represent the structural
change and the behavioural response that policy induces \citep{dyner2001}. Recent
work has begun to address the consequences rather than the optimum: how market
design affects resilience under capacity expansion \citep{pan2026}, how the
representation of hydropower in capacity expansion models changes the result
\citep{oberg2026}, and how the cost of capital --- not resource quality or
levelised cost --- determines which Latin American projects actually get financed
\citep{delrio2026}. The last of these is directly relevant to Peru: it argues that
the binding constraint in the region is institutional and financial rather than
physical.

\subsection{Infrastructure delays and power system reliability}
\label{sec:lit:delays}

A transmission project that is planned but late is, for the period of the delay,
indistinguishable from a project that was never planned. The literature on this is
smaller than the TEP literature but consistent in its findings.
\citet{ritter2019} simulate delayed interconnector expansion in a high-renewable
European system and show that the delay does not merely postpone benefits: it
raises emissions and generation costs, because surplus renewable energy that cannot
be moved is replaced by conventional generation elsewhere, altering the final
composition of the mix. \citet{herrera2019} reach a structurally similar conclusion
for northeast Brazil, where transmission constraints are shown to bound wind
expansion directly. The mechanism in both cases is the same: generation investment
responds to a connection opportunity that the network has not yet delivered.

The causes of delay are well documented and largely non-technical. Right-of-way
disputes, licensing, and local opposition recur across jurisdictions, and
\citet{sun2026} quantify the last of these, showing that mitigating local
opposition materially changes the feasible expansion path. \citet{boateng2026}
generalise the point, identifying institutional-quality thresholds below which
renewable deployment responds very differently to the same incentives. For a system
such as Peru's, where the auction mechanism that structured renewable procurement
has been suspended, these findings suggest that lead-time risk is better modelled
as an institutional variable than as an engineering one.

What is less settled is how much delay actually matters relative to the other
things a planner does not know. Studies typically impose a delay scenario and
report its consequences, which establishes that the effect exists without
establishing its rank. That is the question this paper addresses.

\subsection{Renewable integration and grid expansion in emerging economies}
\label{sec:lit:emerging}

Emerging economies face the integration problem with a different constraint set:
capital is more expensive, institutions are thinner, and the grid is often radial
or longitudinal rather than meshed. \citet{dasilva2025} analyse the regional
efficiency of renewable expansion in Brazil and find performance varying sharply by
region, which is consistent with network access rather than resource quality being
the differentiator. \citet{antoniolli2022} document the role of residential rooftop
prosumers in Brazil, a mechanism that reduces the inter-zonal transfers a
longitudinal grid must support, and \citet{wu2025} reach a similar conclusion for
community-scale rooftop solar and wind. \citet{sheng2026} make the link explicit by
planning urban rooftop PV specifically to relieve grid-congestion-induced
curtailment.

The alternative to building network is to use the existing network harder. Grid-
enhancing technologies --- dynamic line rating, power-flow control, advanced
monitoring --- raise effective transfer capability without new right-of-way, and
\citet{sadeghian2026} formalise dynamic thermal line rating under chance
constraints for precisely this purpose. Their appeal in a system such as Peru's is
obvious, since the constraint is corridor capacity rather than generation
adequacy; their limitation is that remuneration frameworks for them are generally
absent, so deployment lags technical feasibility.

Storage plays the complementary role. \citet{sousa2024} quantify the substitution
between storage and other flexibility resources in the Portuguese system, and
\citet{liu2024} integrate multi-time-scale uncertainty and low-carbon retrofit into
a regional capacity expansion scheme. Both make the same structural point: storage
and network are partial substitutes, so the amount of transmission a system needs
is not independent of how much flexibility it builds.

\subsection{System dynamics in energy planning}
\label{sec:lit:sd}

System dynamics takes the opposite methodological stance to optimisation. Rather
than solving for the best configuration, it simulates the configuration that the
system's own feedback structure, accumulations and delays will produce
\citep{forrester1997,sterman2000}. For electricity this has proved productive
because the pathologies of interest --- investment cycles, capacity gaps, price
spikes following construction lags --- are consequences of structure rather than of
optimisation failure.

The lineage is long. \citet{ford2001} explained the California capacity crisis as a
construction-delay phenomenon; \citet{arango2007} simulated alternative regulation
in the Colombian market; \citet{assili2008} examined capacity payments under
long-term planning uncertainty, explicitly incorporating construction delays and
demand growth; and \citet{simshauser2010} connected resource adequacy to investment
uncertainty in a liberalised market. More recent work has extended the approach to
full transition pathways: \citet{zapata2018} analysed the long-run effects of a
100\,\% renewable target on the Colombian market, \citet{morcillo2018} examined
demand-growth scenarios, and \citet{luo2024} built a hybrid system dynamics model
of power-mix trajectories under carbon and capacity policy. \citet{lin2026} apply
the method to the carbon border adjustment mechanism, and \citet{oyarzun2026}
assess residential PV adoption policy with a system dynamics model --- an
indication that the approach remains current in this journal's literature.

Model confidence in system dynamics is established by a documented battery of tests
rather than by a goodness-of-fit statistic \citep{barlas1996,oliva2004}, and recent
methodological work has reconsidered what confidence means for models used
exploratorily rather than predictively \citep{richardson2024}. We follow that
protocol explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} and report it in full in the
supplementary material, because a model used to rank uncertainties must first be
shown to be structurally sound.

Where the system dynamics energy literature is thinnest is in uncertainty
quantification. Scenario analysis is near-universal; designed sampling of the input
space is rare. Outside system dynamics the tools are standard --- Latin hypercube
sampling \citep{mckay1992} and variance-based or regression-based global
sensitivity measures \citep{homma1996} --- and they are used in power system work,
for instance in \citeauthor{alvarez2026}'s \citeyearpar{alvarez2026} probabilistic
assessment of the Spanish 2030 system by Monte Carlo analysis. Combining them with
a feedback-rich simulation model is the methodological step this paper takes.

\subsection{Latin American and Peruvian electricity system studies}
\label{sec:lit:latam}

Peru's restructuring has been studied as a reference case. \citet{rudnick2019}
document a reform that delivered competition, lower wholesale prices and improved
security of supply, while noting the accumulation of a high reserve margin and
increasing fossil dependence; \citet{perezreyes2009} measure the productivity gains
in distribution following the reforms, which were real but concentrated. More
recent work has turned to the transition itself. \citet{fiestas2024} quantify the
system-level impact of raising non-conventional renewables in the Peruvian matrix,
finding reduced gas consumption, avoided emissions and lower system costs, and
\citet{delacruz2024} reach compatible conclusions in long-range demand and
emissions scenarios to 2050. On the network side, \citet{valdivia2024} report the
congestion-analysis practice now used in Peruvian transmission planning, including
$n-1$ contingency screening with demand, generation, fuel-price and weather
projections.

At regional scale, \citet{sauma2006} established the proactive-versus-reactive
distinction in transmission valuation that frames much of the subsequent debate,
and analyses of Andean interconnection have long found the links technically and
economically feasible. The obstacle identified is political rather than
engineering: regulatory sovereignty, not transfer capability
\citep{guliev2022,sempertegui2017}. Peru's position in the regional comparison is
genuinely ambiguous --- early policy innovation through auctions, substantial
resources, and yet no consolidated decarbonisation strategy
\citep{campodonico2022,reyes2025,colinacalvo2024,torres2026}.

\subsection{Research gap and positioning}
\label{sec:lit:gap}

Three gaps follow from the above.

First, the delay literature establishes that lead times matter but not how much
they matter relative to other uncertainties. Delay is typically imposed as a
scenario; it is rarely ranked against demand, fuel prices or cost assumptions in a
common design.

Second, the system dynamics energy literature rarely quantifies uncertainty with a
designed experiment. The strength of the method is that it reproduces
counter-intuitive behaviour arising from structure; the corresponding weakness, when
it is used for policy, is that a small number of named scenarios cannot separate a
structural result from an artefact of one input assumption.

Third, Peru is under-represented. The existing Peruvian studies are either
optimisation-based assessments of a renewable target
\citep{fiestas2024,delacruz2024} or operational analyses of the network
\citep{valdivia2024}. None represents the investment-and-delay feedback between
generation and transmission that a longitudinal, gas-dependent system with
suspended auctions would be expected to exhibit.

This paper addresses all three: a system dynamics model of the Peruvian system with
explicit generation and transmission pipelines, subjected to a Latin-hypercube
designed experiment that ranks four uncertainties against each other on a common
footing, with the validation battery and the full input set reported.
